Dead Reckoning

The visual language of a naval command exercise. Everything here is taken from the running application rather than drawn up beside it — if a value appears on this page it is the value the app uses. The system has one job: to keep a glass instrument panel legible over a live, moving, brightly lit sea.

Principles

Five rules the rest of the system follows.

The picture comes first Chrome is glass laid over a scene that never stops moving. It borrows the scene's light, never competes with it, and occupies the edges.
Two sides, two colours Teal is ours. Amber is theirs. No element uses both, and nothing else earns an accent colour — a third hue would make the first two meaningless.
One typeface, many weights of silence A single monospace family throughout. Hierarchy is made with size, letter-spacing and opacity — never with a second family or a bold weight.
Round for controls, soft-square for surfaces Anything you press is a circle or a capsule. Anything that holds information is a rounded rectangle. The shape tells you whether it is for reading or for touching.
Legibility is engineered Type over the scene gets a scrim or a two-stage shadow. It is never left to chance, because the sea behind it changes by the second.

The two sides

The whole information design rests on this pair. Teal is the friendly board, the grid, the confirmed state; amber is the enemy, the hit, the burning hull. A control that is on glows teal; a hull that is gone glows amber.

Theatres

Three lighting states drive the scene, and the interface sits on all three without changing. Each is a full palette — sun, zenith, mid sky, haze, deep water, shoal — cross-faded over 0.8 s when the theatre changes.

The orders block is the one reading surface tinted teal rather than neutral: it reads back a set of choices the reader has just made, so it belongs to their side of the board. Labels sit at 38% and the values at 86% — the eye should land on what was chosen, not on what the rows are called.

The hull roster draws each enemy ship flat, at the length the plot says it is — one segment per cell, filled where a salvo has gone home, the whole outline turning amber once she is down. It answers what a list of grid references cannot: how much of each ship is still unaccounted for.

Telling the outcome

One word carries the whole after-action screen, and the two sides' colours do the saying: teal when the plot was hers, amber when it was theirs. The rules either side draw outward from the word and stop short of the edge — they frame it rather than underline it. The figures change with the verdict as well as the words: a defeat that reports a good day's shooting reads as a bug, not a defeat.

A stat cell is icon, figure, label — in that order, centred, with the icon at 44% and the label at 50%, so the figure is the only thing that carries weight. A cell turns amber only when the figure is a loss of her own.

Saying how a thing is worked

An instruction floats over the thing it is about, not in the panel beside it. It is a capsule like any other control, but it takes no pointer: nothing here is pressable, and a hint that looks pressable is a lie. A segmented switch floats the same way and is pressable — two segments rather than one toggle, because a lone button has to choose between naming what you are looking at and naming what you would get, and either reading is a guess. Each segment keeps its own side's colour, so the switch says whose board is up without being read.

Legibility over the scene

The hardest problem in the system. Type sits on a sea that runs from near-black at night to a pale haze at noon, so contrast cannot be left to the colour alone.

Scrim Where large type meets the sky — the title screen — a vertical gradient from rgba(5,8,14,.80) to transparent over the top 44% of the frame. Against the Squall haze this takes the title from roughly 2:1 to better than 5:1.
Two-stage shadow Small type over the scene carries 0 1px 2px rgba(4,7,13,.95) for a tight rim and 0 0 18px rgba(4,7,13,.9) for a soft ground. The rim is what holds it over light water; the glow is what holds it over dark.
Glass, not shadow Anything with more than three words gets a panel instead. A shadow can rescue a label; it cannot rescue a paragraph.
Opacity floor 46% on glass, 28% reserved for section headings that sit directly above the value they name.

Format

The application is composed for an iPad mini held upright — 744 × 1133, ratio 0.657. Everything is a child of a single stage element at that size, so the whole interface can be re-hosted at any dimension without touching a rule.

LayoutSplitWhere
Whole744 × 1133Title, deployment, after action. One block, centred, with the title above it.
Two boards744 × 566 eachBattle. The enemy plot above on a fixed plan view, her own formation below on the orbit rig — two passes into one target. The board nearest the controls is the one the hand moves.
Board and panel566 above · 567 belowSplit deployment and split after action. The lower half is the panel itself, never a card floating inside it — a card in a half that small has no room to be a card.

A half-screen panel pads 20 / 26 / 62: the foot is deep because the Screens and Debug pills live there, and an action flush with the bottom would sit under them. Slack inside the panel is split by an auto margin at each end rather than pooled into one hole above the buttons.

An instrument beside a board, never across it. The upper board on the battle screen is framed to 419 × 418 rather than filling its half, which opens a gutter of about 160 either side; the readout lives there in two columns of 120, clear of the grid by 20. A bar across the foot covered the bottom row of cells, and a plot you cannot read is not a plot.

A card that narrow wraps rather than spills: every row folds onto a second line before anything is squeezed out or clipped. Labels sit under their figures when they will not sit beside them.

A board seen in plan has a square bounding box, so in a half it is the height that binds: 487 × 487 inside 744 × 566, dropped clear of the label band rather than centred in the half.
